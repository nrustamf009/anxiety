# Implicit Bible passages on anxiety (lament, narratives of distress) and short Christian contemplative practices

Research notes for a Christian anxiety-help app (final report in Russian). Numbering convention: **Syn** = Russian Synodal (Синодальный перевод); **Eng** = English (Hebrew/Masoretic-based) numbering. In Psalms, Synodal follows the LXX numbering, which is usually one lower than English from Ps 10 to Ps 147 (e.g. Eng Ps 42 = Syn Ps 41). 1-2 Samuel = 1-2 Царств; 1-2 Kings = 3-4 Царств.

Method note: about 15 searches. Sources reached include PubMed and BMJ (Bernardi 2001), book reviews of Vroegop, ignatianspirituality.com, desiringgod.org (Lloyd-Jones/Piper), Christian Meditation Lab at California Baptist University (Knabb), and azbyka.ru listings. Several hoped-for sources could not be fetched or were not found: the Lopukhin commentary on 3 Kings 19, St Ignatius (Brianchaninov)'s exact wording on breathing, a Templeton-funded Hesychast prayer RCT page (host blocked), and Sefaria/Breslov material on Ps 88. These are listed under Gaps. Where the notes describe what a passage says, the Bible text itself is the source. Interpretive claims without a fetched source are marked as inferences.

## Q1. Which implicit passages give "permission" to feel anxiety and model healthy responses?

### Takeaway
Scripture contains many narratives and prayers in which faithful people (including Jesus) feel dread, despair, panic or a wish to die, and God responds with presence, care for the body, and honest dialogue rather than rebuke. The strongest "permission" texts are Gethsemane (Matt 26 / Luke 22), Elijah (Syn 3 Цар 19 / Eng 1 Kings 19), Ps 42-43 (Syn 41-42), Ps 88 (Syn 87), Ps 13 (Syn 12) and Paul in 2 Cor 1:8-10. The healthy responses they model are: telling God the truth, attending to sleep and food, talking back to one's own soul, asking companions for support, remembering God's past acts, and accepting that the lament may not resolve today.

### Cited Findings

**Overall framing (lament as legitimate faith-speech)**
- Walter Brueggemann sorts the psalms into psalms of *orientation*, *disorientation* and *new orientation*, with two "moves of faith": from settled trust into disorientation, and from disorientation toward a new coherence. — [Working Preacher, four-part Psalms series](https://www.workingpreacher.org/?print-all=31569); [Review of Brueggemann, *Spirituality of the Psalms*](https://mcsletstalk.org/reclaiming-lament/book-review-spirituality-of-the-psalms/)
- Brueggemann treats Ps 88 as the most extreme disorientation psalm. It has no closing turn to trust, thanksgiving or praise and ends in darkness. Commentators note that his scheme should be "held loosely" and not forced onto all 150 psalms. — [Working Preacher](https://www.workingpreacher.org/?print-all=31569); [WPC sermon notes on Ps 88](https://wpc.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/March2Jones2025.pdf)
- A scholarly article on Ps 88 argues that the psalm "gives space and permission for sufferers to safely protest with a fellow-sufferer." — [Scriptura journal article on Ps 88](https://scriptura.journals.ac.za/pub/article/view/2070)
- Job and the lament psalmists (e.g. Job 10:1 and Ps 88) "speak exactly how they feel, yet always direct their words toward God, not away from Him." — [Bible Hub Q&A on Job 10:1 and the laments](https://biblehub.com/q/Job_10_1_s_link_to_Psalms_laments.htm)
- Jewish reading: a NachDaily summary (Rabbi Shaya Sussman) says Ps 88 was written about the Jewish nation in exile and keeps a somber tone throughout, and that a person "ridden with suffering might find comfort here." — via search summary of OU Torah material ([OU Torah PDF](https://res.cloudinary.com/ouinternal/image/upload/v1784545519/outorah%20pdf/llmlrltz5dxb6bvwwqbz.pdf)); Hebrew/English text: [Jewish Virtual Library, Tehillim 88](https://www.jewishvirtuallibrary.org/tehillim-psalms-chapter-88)

### Inferences
- The narratives share a common pattern: honest expression → God's presence/care (often bodily) → a reframed perspective → a next step. This maps onto both the lament form and CBT sequences (notice → name → reappraise → act).
- For an anxious Christian, the most "permission-giving" texts are where a *faithful* figure feels the emotion without being rebuked: Jesus (Gethsemane), Elijah (fed, not scolded), Paul (2 Cor 1:8). These should come first in the app to reduce shame ("am I sinning by being anxious?").
- Ps 88 and Job act as a guard against "toxic positivity" in the app. Not every session needs a happy ending.
- The app should avoid framing anxiety as simply "unbelief" (Lloyd-Jones's phrase). Lloyd-Jones himself acknowledged temperament and physical factors ([TGC](https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/blogs/justin-taylor/talk-dont-listen-to-yourself/)).

## Q2. What are the stages/structure of lament and how can an app guide users through it?

### Takeaway
The most app-friendly structure is Mark Vroegop's four movements, **Turn → Complain → Ask → Trust** (Повернуться → Пожаловаться → Попросить → Довериться), drawn from Ps 10, 13, 22 and 77. It sits within Brueggemann's broader orientation → disorientation → new orientation arc, and the order of steps is flexible. Ps 13 (Syn 12) is the ideal 6-verse model. Ps 88 (Syn 87) shows that the "trust" step may be minimal or absent on a given day.

### Cited Findings
- Vroegop (*Dark Clouds, Deep Mercy*, Crossway 2019) captures the lament pattern in four words, "turn, complain, ask and trust" (p. 23), drawn from Ps 10, 13, 22, 77. — [9Marks review](https://www.9marks.org/?p=35007); [ERLC review](https://erlc.com/resource/do-we-know-what-to-do-with-our-pain-and-grief/); [TGC conference media](https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/conference_media/dark-clouds-deep-mercy/)
- Step definitions in the reviews: **Turn** = turning to God in prayer, addressing Him at the outset. **Complain** = bringing honest grievances before God. **Ask** = petitioning God boldly. **Trust** = choosing to trust God's goodness while the difficulty remains. — [Sonderbooks review](https://sonderbooks.com/blog/?p=45554); [Servants of Grace review](https://servantsofgrace.org/dark-clouds-deep-mercy-mark-vroegop/)
- One reviewer notes that Vroegop "pulls out four elements and doesn't worry about the order," in contrast to a six-part lament form used elsewhere. — [search summary of review, MTS](https://mts.com.au/?p=8993)
- The book has three parts: learning to lament, what lament teaches, and living with lament. A devotional journal version exists. — [Servants of Grace](https://servantsofgrace.org/dark-clouds-deep-mercy-mark-vroegop/); [Dark Clouds Deep Mercy Devotional Journal](https://store.faithlafayette.org/all-products/dark-clouds-deep-mercy-devotional-journal/)
- Brueggemann: orientation → disorientation → new orientation. The new orientation does not erase the darkness ("the experience of disorientation is not forgotten"). — [Working Preacher](https://www.workingpreacher.org/?print-all=31569); [Review of *Spirituality of the Psalms*](https://mcsletstalk.org/reclaiming-lament/book-review-spirituality-of-the-psalms/)
- Empirical: an RCT of a 4-week electronic intervention based on lament psalms (20 devotional readings plus weekly experiential assignments; n = 192 students at Christian institutions; *Journal of Psychology and Theology*, with Mark McMinn among the authors) did **not** confirm its main predictions. Participants with maximum compliance showed higher "Communion with God." — [relbib record, "Resolving Anger toward God: Lament as an avenue toward Attachment"](https://relbib.de/Record/1809026539/Details)
- A Murdoch University PhD examined the psychodynamic effects of lament psalms used as ritual prayer for personal distress. — [Murdoch research portal](https://researchportal.murdoch.edu.au/esploro/outputs/doctoral/An-examination-of-the-psychodynamic-effects/991005543952507891)
- A 2019 *Verbum et Ecclesia* article states that empirical studies show lament can help individuals and communities heal. — [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/09eb7febe8fa4b3699f2e259eaa3b900)

### Inferences (proposed app flow, 3-5 minutes)
1. **Повернись (Turn)**: a prompt such as "Обратись к Богу по имени: Господи… / Отче…" plus one slow breath.
2. **Пожалуйся (Complain)**: free text, "What is happening? What do you feel in your body? What are you afraid of?" No filtering. Show Ps 12:2-3 (Syn) / Ps 13:1-2 "Доколе?" as permission.
3. **Попроси (Ask)**: "What exactly do you need?" (peace, wisdom, sleep, a specific outcome). Phil 4:6 "открывайте свои желания Богу".
4. **Доверься (Trust)**: choose a truth or memory (Ps 12:6 Syn / 13:5 Eng; Lam 3:22-23). Offer an explicit "I can't get to trust today" option that links to Ps 87 (Syn) / 88 (Eng).
5. Optional: save the lament and return in a week to see what changed. This mirrors Brueggemann's "new orientation" and the Examen's review.
- The weak RCT result (main effects not confirmed; compliance-dependent) suggests the app should present lament as a spiritual practice with modest, unproven mental-health effects, and should encourage regular use.

### Gaps
- Did not access Vroegop's book directly. The step definitions come from reviews.
- Did not obtain Claus Westermann's classic lament-form elements (address, complaint, confession of trust, petition, vow of praise) from a fetched source. These are well-known but unverified here.
- Full results and effect sizes of the lament RCT were not obtained.

## Q3. What specific short practices are well-grounded in both tradition and evidence?

### Takeaway
The best-supported short practice is **rhythmic repetitive prayer at about 6 breaths/minute**, i.e. the Jesus Prayer or a breath prayer, or the rosary. It is rooted in Orthodox hesychasm and Catholic devotion. Bernardi et al. (BMJ 2001) showed that the rosary recited in Latin slows breathing to about 6/min and raises baroreflex sensitivity. Knabb & Vazquez (2018) found in a small RCT that an online Jesus Prayer program reduced daily stress. Centering prayer, lectio divina and the Examen have only pilot-level or indirect evidence. Gratitude, Scripture memorization and Sabbath are traditionally rich, but no specific trials were collected here.

### Cited Findings

**Rosary / mantra breathing: Bernardi et al., BMJ 2001**
- Bernardi L, et al. "Effect of rosary prayer and yoga mantras on autonomic cardiovascular rhythms: comparative study." *BMJ* 2001;323(7327):1446-9. doi:10.1136/bmj.323.7327.1446. 23 healthy adults, Florence and Pavia. — [PubMed 11751348](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11751348/); [BMJ](https://www.bmj.com/content/323/7327/1446); [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC61046)
- Reciting the Ave Maria (Latin rosary) or the mantra "om-mani-padme-om" slowed breathing to about 6 breaths/min. It produced "striking, powerful, and synchronous increases" in cardiovascular rhythms. Baroreflex sensitivity rose from 9.5 (SD 4.6) to 11.5 (4.9) ms/mmHg, P<0.05, similar to controlled breathing at 6/min. The authors conclude that rhythm formulas at about 6 breaths/min "induce favourable psychological and possibly physiological effects." — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11751348/)
- Limitation: a small physiological study in healthy volunteers, with no anxiety outcome measured. — same source; inference

**Jesus Prayer (Orthodox hesychast tradition)**
- Knabb, J., & Vazquez, V. (2018). "A randomized controlled trial of a two-week Internet-based contemplative prayer program for Christians with daily stress." *Spirituality in Clinical Practice*, 5, 37-53. The Jesus Prayer was practiced formally and informally for 2 weeks. The Jesus Prayer group (n = 44) outperformed wait-list (n = 42) on stress and on surrender as religious coping, with within- and between-group differences. — [scite author page, V. Vazquez](https://scite.ai/authors/veola-e-vazquez-nOj41N); [Christian Psychologist podcast, ep. 15 Stress](https://the-christian-psychologist-mental-health-for-21st-century-c.simplecast.com/episodes/episode-15-stress-oGq_YS_N)
- Counter-evidence: a 2025 Liberty University dissertation RCT of the Jesus Prayer combined with breathing found **no significant effect** on self-reported public-speaking anxiety or on HRV (RMSSD). — [Liberty University Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.liberty.edu/doctoral/7353)

**Knabb's broader Christian meditation program**
- Knabb et al. (2020). "Christian meditation for repetitive negative thinking: A multi-site randomized trial examining the effects of a four-week preventative program." *Spirituality in Clinical Practice*, 7, 34-50. The program taught kataphatic (words/images) and apophatic (wordless) Christian meditation, compared with wait-list at two Christian universities. Reported medium effects on surrender to God and large effects on nonattachment and perseverative thinking (from a summary; not verified in the full paper). — [AACC course listing](https://aacc.net/product/420-christian-meditation-for-repetitive-negative-thinking-theory-research-and-applications-3/); [CBU Christian Meditation Lab publications](https://calbaptist.edu/college-of-behavioral-and-social-sciences/center-study-human-behavior/labs/christian-meditation-lab/publications-and-presentations)

**Centering prayer**
- A 2016 pilot (Fox et al., *Mental Health, Religion & Culture*; n = 9, 6 weeks, no control group) reported decreased anxiety and stress and increased mindfulness and faith development. — [Informa/T&F](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/13674676.2016.1203299); [relbib record](https://relbib.de/Record/1838998543/Details)
- A 2024 *Pastoral Psychology* study (7-week undergraduate course with centering prayer vs. another religion course) found trends toward decreased anxiety and increased hope and compassion, but **no significant changes**. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11089-023-01103-w)
- Dorais & Gutierrez (2021, *Frontiers in Psychology*) RCT: a 4-week online "centering meditation" based on centering prayer improved stress and trait mindfulness in college students. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.720824/epub)

### Inferences: pairing practices with evidence-based techniques (1-5 min)
| Practice (tradition) | Pairs with (evidence-based technique) | 1-5 min app format |
|---|---|---|
| **Jesus Prayer / breath prayer** ("Господи Иисусе Христе, Сыне Божий, помилуй мя грешного": inhale on the first half, exhale on the second) | Slow diaphragmatic breathing at about 6/min (cf. Bernardi 2001) | Animated breath pacer: 5 s inhale / 5 s exhale, 3 minutes. Short forms: "Господи, помилуй"; Ps 55:4 Syn / 56:3 Eng "Когда я в страхе, на Тебя я уповаю". Orthodox caution: the tradition emphasises attention of mind and heart, and the Fathers warned beginners against mechanical bodily/breathing techniques without guidance. A note in the app should say "breathing pacing is a help, not the essence of prayer" (unverified quotation; see Gaps). |
| **Rosary decade** (Catholic) | Same 6/min respiratory pacing | One decade at a slow pace, about 3-4 min. |
| **Lament (Turn-Complain-Ask-Trust)** | Expressive writing; emotion labeling | Guided 4-screen journaling (see Q2). |
| **"Why are you cast down, O my soul" self-talk (Ps 41-42 Syn)** | Cognitive reframing / restructuring | Thought record: thought → "О чём унываешь, душа?" → evidence → truth from Scripture. |
| **Ignatian Examen** | Worry time / scheduled reflection; gratitude journaling | 5-minute evening Examen. Step 3 (feelings) can hold "parked" worries, and Step 5 makes a single next-step plan. |
| **Gratitude & thanksgiving** (Phil 4:6 "с благодарением") | Gratitude interventions | "3 благодарности" before the request. |
| **Lectio divina** (read, meditate, pray, contemplate) on a short verse | Mindful attention, decentering from rumination (Knabb's "notice-shift-accept-act") | 4-minute guided lectio on one verse (e.g. Мф 11:28; Ис 41:10). |
| **Centering prayer** (sacred word, gently return) | Attention training / mindfulness; acceptance (ACT) | 3-5 min timer with a gentle bell. Note the evidence is weak (pilot or non-significant). |
| **Hagar's "God who sees me" / Elijah's "gentle whisper"** | 5-4-3-2-1 grounding | "Open your eyes like Hagar": name 5 things you see, 4 you hear… then "Ты Бог видящий меня". |
| **Scripture memorization** | Coping statements / cue cards | Spaced-repetition cards (Фил 4:6-7; 1 Пет 5:7; Пс 22 Syn / 23 Eng; Плач 3:22-23). |
| **Sabbath rest** (Elijah's sleep and food) | Behavioral activation, sleep hygiene, self-care check | "Elijah check" (sleep / food / water / company / task size). |

- Overall evidence ranking for the report: (1) slow rhythmic prayer breathing, with physiological evidence and a small RCT for the Jesus Prayer, though there is also a null RCT; (2) Knabb's multi-component Christian meditation programs, with RCTs vs. wait-list only in Christian college samples; (3) centering prayer, lectio and lament, with pilot, mixed or null results; (4) Examen, gratitude-as-prayer, memorization and Sabbath, with strong tradition and indirect secular evidence but no specific trials found here.
- The app should frame these as complements to, not replacements for, professional care. Every RCT found used wait-list controls in non-clinical samples, so they cannot show superiority over secular techniques.

### Gaps
- Did not verify St Ignatius (Brianchaninov)'s or St Theophan's exact warnings about breathing techniques in the Jesus Prayer. Search found the collections but not the passages. Check azbyka.ru «Аскетические опыты», «О молитве Иисусовой», and the *Добротолюбие* (St Gregory Palamas / Nicephorus on "breathing method").
- No empirical studies were found specifically on the Examen, on Scripture memorization, or on Sabbath practice and anxiety.
- No anxiety-disorder (clinical GAD/panic) samples were found for any Christian contemplative practice. The included studies are of healthy volunteers, students or people with daily stress.
- Effect sizes for Knabb 2020 come from a secondary summary. The full paper was not accessed.
- Templeton Hesychastic-prayer RCT results were unavailable because www.templetonworldcharity.org was blocked by the session's network settings.
- Secular evidence for gratitude interventions, 5-4-3-2-1 grounding, and worry-postponement was not researched in this pass (out of scope, presumably covered by other researchers).
