The fetches to calbaptist.edu, uned.es and iocdf.org failed because those hosts are blocked by this session's network settings, so I could not read the Knabb lab publication list, the Ano & Vasconcelles full text or the IOCDF Clergy Packet text. ## Q1. Efficacy of RCBT, Christian-accommodative CBT, and meta-analyses of R/S-adapted psychotherapy vs secular therapy

### Takeaway
R/S-adapted psychotherapy works better than no treatment, but the best-controlled ("additive" or dismantling) comparisons show that its effect on psychological distress is about the same as standard secular therapy (g ≈ 0.13, not significant). Its consistent extra benefit is on spiritual well-being (g ≈ 0.34). Evidence specific to anxiety is thin, small and at high risk of bias. The honest claim is "equivalent for symptoms, possibly preferred by and better for highly religious clients." It is not "superior."

### Cited Findings
- **Koenig et al. 2015 RCBT RCT** (J Nerv Ment Dis, April 2015; DOI 10.1097/NMD.0000000000000273): N = 132 adults with major depression and chronic medical illness, randomized to conventional CBT (n = 67) or religiously integrated CBT (n = 65). Both arms had ten 50-minute sessions over 12 weeks, 94% by telephone. The authors concluded the two were "equivalent treatments" for major depression. Highly religious patients improved somewhat more with RCBT. Limitations: small sample, the trial was not designed or powered as a non-inferiority trial, and the outcome was depression, not anxiety. — [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2015/03/150331145017.htm); [News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20150401/Researchers-evaluate-effects-of-integrating-religious-beliefs-into-depression-therapy.aspx); [Duke Scholars record](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/823695)
- The RCBT manual (Pearce, Koenig et al. 2015, *Psychotherapy*, DOI 10.1037/a0036448) has versions for Christian, Jewish, Muslim, Hindu and Buddhist clients. Its core technique is challenging distorted thoughts with scripture, plus prayer, gratitude, altruism and forgiveness. Duke's Center for Spirituality, Theology and Health distributes it free. **[unverified this session]**
- An RCBT secondary analysis reported effects of religious vs conventional CBT on purpose in life in the same trial population. — [IJEPP article](https://www.meaning.ca/ijepp-article/vol6-no1/effects-of-religious-vs-conventional-cognitive-behavioral-therapy-on-purpose-in-life-in-clients-with-major-depression-and-chronic-medical-illness-a-randomized-clinical-trial-2/)
- **Captari et al. 2018 meta-analysis** (J Clin Psychol 74(11); DOI 10.1002/jclp.22681): 97 outcome studies, N = 7,181. R/S-adapted therapy versus no treatment and other therapies: psychological g = 0.74, spiritual g = 0.74. Versus non-R/S psychotherapies: psychological g = 0.33, spiritual g = 0.43. In **additive/dismantling designs**, the most rigorous comparison where the same therapy is run with and without R/S content, the R/S version was equal on psychological distress (g = 0.13, p = 0.258) and better on spiritual well-being (g = 0.34). These figures come partly from secondary or truncated abstract text, so verify them against the DOI. — [Zendy record](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/jclp.22681); [PubMed record via search](https://searchworks.jioinstitute.edu.in/articles/cmedm__30221353)
- A 2024 meta-analysis of RCTs reported R/S treatments moderately more efficacious than regular treatments: g = 0.52 at post-treatment and g = 0.72 at follow-up. It used different inclusion criteria from Captari and the comparator definitions need checking. — [PubMed 37615090](https://www.pubmed.gov/37615090/)
- **Anderson et al. 2015 meta-analysis** (J Affect Disord; DOI 10.1016/j.jad.2015.01.019): systematic review of RCTs of faith-adapted CBT (F-CBT) for depression and anxiety, 16 eligible studies out of 2,274 citations. F-CBT for depression beat controls. In the Christian-CBT subgroup the pooled SMD was −0.59 (95% CI −0.95 to −0.23), a moderate effect. Risk of bias (Cochrane tool) was high. **Anxiety studies could not be pooled**: three studies reported F-CBT significantly outperforming controls, and the authors said "no firm conclusions" were possible. — [White Rose accepted manuscript](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/85676); [PubMed 25723562](https://www.pubmed.gov/25723562/); [Mental Elf critique](https://www.nationalelfservice.net/treatment/cbt/faith-based-cbt-for-depression-and-anxiety-review-highlights-a-lack-of-good-quality-evidence/)
- **Gonçalves et al. 2015 meta-analysis** (Psychol Med 45(14):2937–2949; DOI 10.1017/S0033291715001166): 23 RCTs of R/S interventions. Significant effects on general anxiety symptoms (p < 0.001), including meditation-based (p < 0.001) and psychotherapy-based (p = 0.02) subgroups. Anxiety was the main symptom that improved. Depression effects were weaker, with only marginal differences at 1–6 months (p = 0.05). Protocols were heterogeneous and unstandardized, and there were few good-quality trials. — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/psychological-medicine/article/religious-and-spiritual-interventions-in-mental-health-care-a-systematic-review-and-metaanalysis-of-randomized-controlled-clinical-trials/B26314DC89133A3FA4CC4220B6A5FBCF); [PubMed 26200715](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26200715/)
- Background **[unverified this session]**: Hook, Worthington, Davis et al. 2010 (J Clin Psychol 66(1):46–72) reviewed 24 R/S therapies against Chambless & Hollon criteria. Christian-accommodative CBT for depression was rated "probably efficacious"; no R/S therapy for anxiety met the "efficacious" threshold. Earlier work by Worthington, Hook, Davis & McDaniel (2011) found R/S therapies outperformed secular ones on spiritual outcomes but not reliably on psychological ones once rigor was controlled.

### Inferences
- The defensible marketing and clinical claim is that faith-integrated CBT is about as effective as secular CBT for symptoms, adds spiritual-wellbeing benefits, and may improve acceptability and engagement for devout users. Claims of superiority over standard treatment are not supported.
- The active ingredient is likely the CBT/behavioral core, such as cognitive restructuring and behavioral activation. The religious framing works as cultural accommodation. An app should therefore keep evidence-based CBT techniques and use scripture as a vehicle, not as a replacement.
- Nearly all RCBT data is on **depression**. For anxiety specifically, the app would be extrapolating.

### Gaps
- Exact pooled effect for anxiety-only outcomes in Captari 2018: not confirmed (full text not retrieved).
- No large, pre-registered, non-inferiority RCT of Christian CBT for generalized anxiety disorder found.

## Q2. Christian meditation/prayer, digital faith-based apps, Bible reading and mental health

### Takeaway
Christian contemplative practices (Jesus Prayer, centering prayer, lectio divina) have a small, mostly pilot-level evidence base, largely from one lab (Knabb, California Baptist University) using short online programs with non-clinical samples. Faith-app RCTs (e.g., Pray.com) are registered or completed but not peer-reviewed in independent outlets. Bible-engagement "flourishing" data are cross-sectional surveys from advocacy organizations and cannot show causation.

### Cited Findings
- Knabb & Vazquez (2018, *Spirituality in Clinical Practice*) ran an RCT of a 2-week Internet-based contemplative prayer program for Christians with daily stress. Knabb & Vazquez also report a randomized trial of a 4-week Christian meditation program for adults with repetitive negative thinking (RNT), with outcomes not reported in the listing found. — [AACC workshop listing](https://aacc.net/product/420-christian-meditation-for-repetitive-negative-thinking-theory-research-and-applications-3/); [Knabb et al. 2023, Mindfulness](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s12671-023-02203-7.pdf)
- Knabb & Frederick's *Contemplative Prayer for Christians with Chronic Worry: An Eight-Week Program* (Routledge) includes the Jesus Prayer as a practice for worry. It is a workbook, not a trial. — [Routledge](https://www.routledge.com/Contemplative-Prayer-for-Christians-with-Chronic-Worry-An-Eight-Week-Program/Knabb-Frederick/p/book/9781138690943)
- Knabb lab results as remembered **[unverified this session]**: several small online trials (typically N ≈ 50–150 Christian adults, non-clinical) of 2–4 week Jesus Prayer, "Lord's Prayer" or "loving-kindness toward God" meditations. They reported reductions in worry (PSWQ), stress or RNT versus waitlist, with small-to-moderate effects, short follow-up and self-selected online samples.
- **Pray.com / Biola University RCT (NCT05622604)**: about 312 high-stress adults randomized to app prayer use 5 days a week, a meditative-prayer version, or a waitlist. Primary outcome is the Perceived Stress Scale at 2 months. Listed as completed. A company press release claims more days of app use were associated with lower stress, anxiety and depression. This is sponsor-reported and a dose-response association, not an intention-to-treat effect. — [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05622604); [Press release](https://www.newswire.com/news/new-studies-link-improved-mental-health-to-use-of-digitally-delivered-22319107)
- **Coventry University RCT protocol (NCT06136676; protocol published BMC Psychology 2024)**: three arms comparing app-delivered Christian/Islamic "heart meditation", MBSR and waitlist, with daily 20-minute sessions for 8 weeks. No results yet. — [Coventry pure portal](https://pureportal.coventry.ac.uk/en/publications/interpersonal-and-individual-effects-of-an-app-based-christian-an/); [DOAJ BMC Psychology](https://doaj.org/article/78f033454dad47b4bd1a68f368719bb1)
- Rosmarin et al. 2010 **[unverified this session]**: a web-based spiritually integrated stress-management program for Jewish adults (N ≈ 125) reduced worry and stress versus a progressive muscle relaxation (PMR) comparator and waitlist. It is often cited as a proof of concept for digital religious interventions.
- Bible engagement and flourishing **[unverified this session]**: the Center for Bible Engagement (Back to the Bible) reports that reading scripture 4+ times a week is associated with lower rates of risky behaviors and loneliness. American Bible Society's *State of the Bible* uses a "Human Flourishing" index (adapted from Harvard's Flourishing Program) and reports higher flourishing among "Scripture-engaged" adults. Both are cross-sectional, organization-sponsored surveys. They have obvious confounding from church attendance, social support and conscientiousness, plus reverse causation, and have not been peer-reviewed.
- I found no peer-reviewed efficacy study of the **Abide** app.

### Inferences
- An app can reasonably offer Christian contemplative or meditative exercises as a culturally congruent alternative to secular mindfulness. These have plausible mechanisms (attention retraining, decentering from worry). They should be framed as "supportive practices with early evidence," not as treatment.
- Usage-outcome correlations in sponsor reports are confounded: people who feel better tend to use the app more. Do not cite them as proof of efficacy.

### Gaps
- Exact Knabb trial effect sizes and sample sizes (lab site blocked).
- Published outcomes of NCT05622604.
- Any centering prayer RCT with a clinical anxiety sample: none found.

## Q3. Religious coping (RCOPE / Brief RCOPE), meta-analyses, spiritual struggles

### Takeaway
Positive religious coping (seeking God's support, benevolent reappraisal) is modestly associated with better adjustment. Negative religious coping and spiritual struggles (feeling punished or abandoned by God, demonic reappraisal, doubt, moral struggle) are associated with more anxiety and depression. The struggle–anxiety link runs in both directions: anxiety also feeds struggles. An app must avoid generating negative religious coping, such as "God is punishing you."

### Cited Findings
- **Ano & Vasconcelles 2005** (J Clin Psychol 61(4):461–480; DOI 10.1002/jclp.20049): meta-analysis of 49 studies with 105 effect sizes. Positive coping was related to positive adjustment, and negative coping to negative adjustment (anxiety, depression, distress). — [Zendy record](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/jclp.20049); [data.zhenzhi summary](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/872810332)
  - Pooled correlations **[unverified this session; full text blocked]**: positive coping with positive adjustment r ≈ .33; positive coping with negative adjustment r ≈ −.12; negative coping with negative adjustment (including anxiety and depression) r ≈ .22; negative coping with positive adjustment approximately null (r ≈ −.02).
- **Bockrath, Pargament, Exline et al. 2021 meta-analysis** (Psychol Relig Spiritual; DOI 10.1037/rel0000400): 32 longitudinal studies. R/S struggles predicted worse later psychological adjustment. Anxiety-specific effect sizes not retrieved. — [Pargament site PDF](https://kennethpargament.com/_files/ugd/bc40fb_9b0350ce9a704b62b5af34acc5d69477.pdf); [MIDUS PDF citing it](https://midus.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/2836.pdf)
- **Beeman et al. 2024** (J Affect Disord Reports): in psychiatric outpatients, overall R/S struggles and anxiety predicted each other over time, after controlling for baseline. Moral and ultimate-meaning struggles predicted higher later anxiety. — [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/66d938d7b2af4aa3bdc6fee96bdb90d8)
- **Lindberg, Grubbs, Pargament, Wilt, Exline 2015**: anxiety predicted increases in doubt-related struggles at two weeks, one month and one year. Evidence that doubt predicts later anxiety was mixed. — [Record](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/edsair,edsair.doi...........1e05f7f9c6579264e65466ddde6ac81a)
- Zarzycka & Zietek 2018: in a cross-sectional study, religious struggle correlated positively with anxiety and negatively with life satisfaction. — [J Relig Health](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10943-018-0598-y.pdf)

### Inferences
- Content should model benevolent appraisals: God's presence, care and lament as legitimate (Psalms of lament). It should avoid punitive appraisals ("your anxiety reveals sin or unbelief").
- Since anxious users are prone to doubt struggles, the app should normalize doubt and struggle and not treat them as failures. Pargament's "Spiritual Struggles" approach, such as the *Winding Road* program, specifically targets struggles **[unverified this session]**.
- Brief RCOPE (14 items) could be used as an optional in-app screen to flag users high in negative religious coping for a gentler pathway or referral.

### Gaps
- Anxiety-specific pooled r for negative coping (not verified).

## Q4. Risks: spiritual bypassing, stigma/shame, scrupulosity/religious OCD, discouraging treatment, religious trauma

### Takeaway
The main documented harms are: (1) church messaging that frames mental illness as sin, lack of faith or demonic, which leads some people to stop medication and to leave church; (2) widespread lay belief that prayer and Bible study **alone** can cure serious mental illness; and (3) for scrupulosity/OCD, verse-based reassurance acting as a compulsion that maintains the disorder. A verse-on-demand anxiety app is structurally at risk on all three.

### Cited Findings
- **LifeWay Research 2013** (telephone survey, N = 1,001 US adults, Sept 6–10, 2013): 35% of Americans agreed that "with just Bible study and prayer, ALONE, people with serious mental illness like depression, bipolar disorder, and schizophrenia could overcome mental illness." Nearly half (48%) of evangelical, fundamentalist or born-again Christians agreed. 68% said they would feel welcome in church if mentally ill. Ed Stetzer worried some Christians treat mental illness as a "character flaw." — [LifeWay Research](https://news.lifeway.com/2013/09/17/half-of-evangelicals-believe-prayer-can-heal-mental-illness/); [Christian Post](https://christianpost.com/news/over-50-percent-of-christians-believe-prayer-bible-reading-alone-can-cure-mental-illness.html)
- **Matthew Stanford (Baylor) 2008** (Mental Health, Religion & Culture): N = 293 Christians who had a mental illness diagnosed by a licensed provider (themselves or a family member). Over 32% were told by their church or pastor that they or their loved one did not really have a mental illness, attributing it to personal sin, lack of faith or demonic involvement. 21% reported attribution to demonic activity. Those dismissed attended church less and reported damaged faith. Stanford noted that people who are told they don't have a mental illness are also being told to stop their medication. — [Baylor Magazine](https://magazine.web.baylor.edu/news/story/2008/mental-illness-often-dismissed-local-church); [Newswise](https://www.newswise.com/articles/study-finds-serious-mental-illness-often-dismissed-by-local-church)
- A qualitative metasynthesis of Christians with mental illness found "imposed spiritualization of mental illness" as a theme in negative church experiences. — [Oxford Psychiatry](https://www.psych.ox.ac.uk/publications/publication_modal/1582044)
- LifeWay 2014 follow-up (pastors and families) **[unverified this session]**: about 66% of Protestant pastors rarely or never speak about mental illness in sermons. Many family members wanted churches to talk about it more openly.
- **Scrupulosity / religious OCD**: an OCD subtype with religious or moral obsessions. Compulsions include asking clergy or loved ones whether one has sinned, repeating prayers and confessing. Reassurance gives brief relief, then doubt returns stronger. The first-line treatment is ERP. Examples of exposures include saying a prayer once without correcting mistakes, combined with response prevention such as not asking a religious leader for reassurance. — [IOCDF Clergy Packet (PDF)](https://iocdf.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Clergy-Packet.pdf) (contents not retrievable in this session; the title and topic list were confirmed by search); [Psych Central](https://psychcentral.com/ocd/scrupulosity-when-ocd-targets-your-religious-and-moral-values); [Rula](https://www.rula.com/blog/religious-ocd/)
- Psych Central attributes to IOCDF an estimate that about 7 in 10 people with OCD benefit from medication or ERP. Not confirmed on the IOCDF site. — [Psych Central](https://psychcentral.com/blog/ocd-and-the-tortures-of-scrupulosity/)
- Spiritual bypassing (Welwood's term; Cashwell et al. in counseling literature) **[unverified this session]**: using spiritual ideas or practices to avoid facing unresolved emotional issues. Examples include premature forgiveness, suppressing anger or fear as "unspiritual", and "just pray more." Empirical measurement is limited (e.g., Fox, Cashwell & Picciotto 2017 Spiritual Bypass Scale). Evidence is mostly theoretical or clinical, not RCT-based.
- Religious trauma: no validated diagnostic category exists. Qualitative and survey literature (e.g., on high-control religious environments) describes anxiety and shame triggered by religious content **[unverified this session]**.

### Inferences
- **OCD/scrupulosity design rule**: an anxiety app whose main loop is "feel anxious → get a comforting verse" works like a reassurance machine. For users with scrupulosity, harm OCD or health anxiety, repeated verse-checking ("am I saved?", "has God forgiven me?") is a compulsion that ERP would ask the person to resist. Mitigations: screen for OCD symptoms (e.g., a short OCI-R or Penn Inventory of Scrupulosity item set), rate-limit or track repeated reassurance requests on the same theme, add psychoeducation that reassurance-seeking maintains OCD, and refer to an ERP-trained (ideally faith-sensitive) therapist.
- **Anti-stigma content rule**: never imply that anxiety equals sin, weak faith or disobedience to "do not be anxious" (Phil 4:6, Matt 6:25–34). Pair these verses with explicit messaging that medication and therapy are compatible with faith and that anxiety is a health condition. This counters the 35–48% "prayer alone" belief and the 32% dismissal finding.
- Include crisis and referral pathways (suicidality, panic, severe impairment) and a statement that the app does not replace professional care.

### Gaps
- No RCT data on harms from Bible-verse apps specifically. Risks are inferred from OCD treatment principles and church-stigma surveys.
- Prevalence of scrupulosity among OCD patients (often cited 5–33%) not verified this session.

## Q5. Anxiety prevalence (WHO / GBD) to frame need

### Takeaway
Anxiety disorders are the world's most common mental disorders: about 301 million people in 2019 (about 4% of the global population), and 359 million in 2021 per WHO's 2025 update. Only about 1 in 4 people in need receive any treatment.

### Cited Findings
- WHO fact sheet (Sept 2023): 301 million people with anxiety disorders in 2019. Anxiety disorders are the most common mental disorders. Only about 1 in 4 people in need (27.6%) receive any treatment. More women than men are affected, and onset is often in childhood or adolescence. — [WHO fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/anxiety-disorders)
- WHO updated fact sheet (Sept 2025): 359 million people with anxiety disorders in 2021. — [WHO fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/anxiety-disorders) (as reported by search; verify current text)
- GBD-based estimate: 4.05% of the global population, about 301 million. — [Middle East Current Psychiatry 2023](https://doaj.org/article/eaf8d05dbb394b169e6ddbb6a86fb8b9)

### Inferences
- The treatment gap (about 72% untreated) is the strongest rationale for a low-barrier digital tool. It also means many users will have untreated clinical anxiety, so referral features matter.

### Gaps
- US-specific prevalence (NIMH: about 19% past-year, 31% lifetime any anxiety disorder) not verified this session.

## Q6. Mental health apps generally: efficacy, engagement drop-off, safety

### Takeaway
Apps have small but real effects on generalized anxiety versus inactive controls (g ≈ 0.26). The effect is not significant versus active controls, and part of it may be a "digital placebo." Real-world retention is extremely low: median 30-day retention is 3.3%. CBT-based content and mood monitoring predict larger anxiety effects.

### Cited Findings
- **Linardon et al. 2024** (World Psychiatry; 176 RCTs): generalized anxiety g = 0.26 (NNT = 12.4) versus controls, and g ≈ 0.11 (not significant) versus active comparators. Social anxiety g = 0.52, OCD symptoms g = 0.51, PTSD g = 0.12. Effects were larger when GAD was the primary target and the app used CBT or mood monitoring. Inactive controls inflated effects, suggesting a "digital placebo." — [Mental Elf summary](https://www.nationalelfservice.net/mental-health/depression/apps-for-depression-and-anxiety-big-new-meta-analysis-supports-effectiveness/); [VU Amsterdam record](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/current-evidence-on-the-efficacy-of-mental-health-smartphoneapps-/)
- Linardon et al. 2023 updated meta-analysis on mindfulness apps for depression and anxiety. — [PDF](https://centerhealthyminds.org/assets/files-publications/Linardon-et-al-2023-The-efficacy-of-mindfulness-apps-on-symptoms-of-depression-and-anxiety-An-updated-meta-analysis-of-randomized-controlled-trials.pdf)
- **Baumel et al. 2019** (JMIR 21(9):e14567): 93 mental health apps (median 100,000 installs). Median 15-day retention was 3.9% and median 30-day retention 3.3%. Mindfulness/meditation apps had 4.7% 30-day retention, peer support 8.9%, trackers 6.1%, and breathing-only apps 0%. — [JMIR](https://www.jmir.org/2019/9/E14567/)
- Safety/quality background **[unverified this session]**: reviews of anxiety apps (e.g., Firth et al. 2017 meta-analysis, g ≈ 0.33 vs controls) and app-store audits found that many apps lack evidence or crisis resources. Some suicide-prevention apps gave wrong crisis numbers (Martinengo et al. 2019, BMC Medicine).

### Inferences
- Expect most users to churn within weeks. Design for brief, self-contained benefit per session, plus gentle re-engagement.
- Embedding structured CBT (worry postponement, cognitive restructuring with scripture, gradual exposure) is more evidence-aligned than passive verse delivery.
- Avoid efficacy claims without an active-control trial.

### Gaps
- No engagement data specific to faith-based mental health apps found.
- Exact Firth 2017 and Martinengo 2019 figures not verified this session.
